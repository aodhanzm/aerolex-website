About

I’m Noah. I design things that teach, then I build them.

I’m a learning experience and game designer. My work sits where a lesson meets a game: the practice loops, stories and interfaces that make someone want to do the next repetition.

I came to this sideways. I studied biology and then bioinformatics, taught myself Japanese and Spanish, and tutored Spanish along the way. Somewhere in there I noticed that the hard part of learning is rarely the explanation. It’s getting someone to come back tomorrow.

01 / What I make

Three projects, one question

Each project on this site started as something I wanted to exist, and each is really the same experiment: what happens to practice when it lives inside a place you like being in?

  • The Brazalete turns Spanish conjugation into a device you wear and turn. It’s public, and you can try it now.
  • The Sanctum turns a journal and a star chart into an estate of seven rooms. You can look around as a guest.
  • Hanyu Courtyard is a Mandarin adventure where review rebuilds an old estate. It’s in private playtesting, so the page shows the design.

Each one has a short design story that says what I decided, what I got wrong first, and what still isn’t built.

02 / How I work

Design first, then build it to find out

I work in a loop that has stayed the same across all three projects. I write down the problem and answer a long round of design questions before anything is built. Those answers become a short contract that says what finished looks like. Then I build in small checkpoints and test each one by using it.

I build with AI as a build partner. In practice that means AI tools write most of the code and generate the concept art, and I do the parts that decide whether the thing is any good: what it should teach, how it should feel, which proposal to reject, and whether the result holds up when I play it. I’d rather say that plainly than let the work imply I hand-wrote every line.

The science training shows up as a habit. I try to separate what I’ve measured from what I only believe, and to say which is which. You’ll see that in the design stories, where most of my claims about learning are labelled as hunches.

03 / Leading a team

A ten-person game for 100 students

Before these projects I spent a year as project manager on The Scene, a Spanish-learning game made with Verbilink. I led a remote team of ten: five language tutors, three engineers and two designers. We took it from a tutorial for younger students to a ten-unit beginner course and a playtest with 100 students.

In that playtest, 87% of the students scored higher on their exams after playing than before. Verbilink ran the measurement. My part was the design and the direction: mapping each grammar point to a conversation or a mission, co-designing the curriculum with the tutors, and setting the visual style the designers worked from.

I still advise the team on art and animation. The game isn’t shown on this site yet. I’ll add it when it’s ready to be seen.

04 / What I’m looking for

Teams I’d like to join

I’m looking for a role as a learning experience designer or educational game designer, on a team that cares whether its product actually teaches. Language apps, learning-game studios and edtech companies are the obvious homes. I’m also drawn to work where an experience has to feel like a place: games, reflective and wellness apps, museums and science communication.

I’m happy as a contractor or an employee, I work remotely from Spain, and I’m glad to travel when a team gets together. I also take on freelance design-and-build projects, which is how I’d suggest we start if you’re curious what I’d make of your problem.
