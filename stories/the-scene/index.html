Design story: game-based learning design

Why I built a story game to teach Spanish conjugation

The Scene started when tutors kept agreeing on one thing: conjugation is the hardest part of Spanish to teach.

A 7-minute read. Updated November 2026.

Where it started

I was working as a Spanish and English tutor at a language tutoring company, and many of our students were learning Spanish. When the tutors talked, we kept landing on the same point. Conjugations and a few grammar patterns were the hardest things for students to learn.

The students already had the company’s resources, so I wondered why the grammar still wasn’t getting easier. I asked the company’s leaders if I could build a tool for these grammar points. Every Spanish tutor would give it to their students to try, and we would collect feedback and improve it.

I offered to lead it. I would meet with the tutors and the leaders, build the website that gave the tool to students, collect feedback from tutors and students, and give the students their exams. The project interested me, so I signed myself up. The company agreed, and it became my full-time job.

The company supplied the AI subscriptions I built with, and those came with usage limits. I couldn’t pick the most advanced model for every small question and then run out. So I learned what each model does best, split the project into parts, planned my own schedule and gave deliverables to the company’s officials. By the end I led a team of ten: five language tutors, three engineers and two UI and UX designers.

Why a story

I started with conjugation, because it was the toughest part. I felt conjugation tables were too passive, so I wanted something active that gave students homework they had to do. That homework was also how I would test my hypothesis, which I describe in the Brazalete design story.

Then I had to decide what kind of game it would live in. Duolingo covers a lot of ground. As far as I can tell, nothing in it is built to make conjugations click, and conjugations were the problem our students had. So the game had to be about Spanish specifically.

I wanted it to be a story. The linguist Stephen Krashen argues that comprehensible input, language you can mostly understand, is the most valuable thing a learner can get. Many people learn their first language through parents reading to them and through stories, and stories feel personal, which seems to help. It’s why people tell you to watch Peppa Pig in the language you’re learning. Kids’ shows repeat the same words slowly. So the game needed to feel personal, like being walked through a situation you could meet in real life.

I made it a visual novel, because players who like interactive games often like the story. A unit puts you in one situation: a person introducing themselves, a person doing chores around the house.

Textbooks do this too. Avancemos, a widely used Spanish textbook, gives each unit a story that its characters tell, but the stories don’t connect to each other. I wanted a connected world, so you could get attached to a character, the way you do in Pokémon. You dislike your rival, so you work harder to beat them.

The player also needed gadgets. A learner who forgets a word won’t pull out notes or a grammar table in the middle of a scene. That would break the moment. Something stylish on the wrist feels cooler, in the way Dora has her backpack and map and Diego has his camera.

A world to explore

I wanted the player to live in a specific world they really want to be in. Skyrim’s Tamriel is fantastical, and that makes people want to see its big, strange places. I wanted that pull for Spanish: a place the player wants to study in. That place is Solmara.

The games I kept thinking of let you wander a world and do small games as the way to move along: Super Mario Sunshine, Sonic Adventure and ClueFinders. Animal Crossing is another inspiration.

Testing the Brazalete first

Before building a full game, we needed to find out whether the Brazalete was fun, engaging and actually helped students learn. That’s why it has its own design story. It’s one piece of the larger game, and it also works on its own.

We didn’t have the people available to make a full custom Spanish-learning game with many characters, a plot and units. So the Brazalete could exist by itself first. The full game is being built now, and I still oversee production.

Built with AI

I designed the tool and thought through the interactions by having AI ask me questions. We answered hundreds of them about what it should look like. I wanted the design to come from my decisions, so every choice needed a reason tied to how students understand the material. If I had let the AI decide, I would have had no evidence behind the result.

Next on my list: a timed mode for the Brazalete, and a unit that shows every way to refer to the past in Spanish, possibly as a character telling a story. The Brazalete story lists what else is planned.
