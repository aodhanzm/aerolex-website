From a star chart to an estate you walk through

The Sanctum is an observatory and a journal for a small circle of friends. This is how it stopped being a tool and became a place.

The problem

It worked, and it felt like a spreadsheet with stars

I started this on 26 October 2026 under the name Astro-Map. My first message about it asked for an interactive model of the solar system linked to my birth chart, with a way to move through the dates of my own life.

Within two days it could do a great deal: transits, a timeline, a journal, tarot readings, spell timing, three traditions for friends with different practices. Every feature worked. And using it felt like filling in forms.

A practice like this is personal and a little ceremonial. I wanted the software to feel like the place where you do it, and what I had was a tool with a theme bolted on.

Key decisions

Four choices that shaped it

01

Make it a place

The first real design decision was to stop adding pages and build an estate. Seven rooms: an observatory, a study, a seasonal courtyard, a temple, a workshop, an engraving studio and a library. Each of the app’s sections became one of them.

The references I kept returning to were places where study and ritual are the same activity: a school of magic, a scholars’ tower, bronze instruments that still work after centuries. That gave me a test for every later choice. If a corner of the app feels out of step with the room it sits in, it gets redesigned or moved.

02

One moment, three views

The observatory shows a single moment three ways at once: a list of every planet, a bronze model of the solar system, and a chart wheel. Select something in one and the other two follow. Move the date and all three move together.

I kept the three views because each answers a different question. The list tells you where things are, the model shows why they appear there, and the wheel shows what that means against your own chart. Tying them to one clock is what makes it feel like an instrument.

03

Ask for an audit early, then ship the answers small

On the third day, at version 0.7, a friend who works as a front-end engineer audited it properly. The audit arrived alongside requests from the friends who use it, and I kept those two sources apart on purpose, because “this is confusing” and “I wish it could” need different responses.

The first fix followed almost at once: drafts that save themselves, so leaving a page no longer erased an unfinished entry. From there the app moved in small numbered versions, from 0.7 on 28 October to 0.36 on 9 November. Each release gets one plain-language line in the changelog and updates only the steps of the tour it touches.

04

Turn pages into furniture

The journal began as a list of entries. It’s now a book lying open on a desk: a timeline on the left page, the entry on the right, and tabs for each subject that travel from one side to the other as you turn through it.

One lesson from getting there: the book has to be painted straight on. A page that turns needs flat geometry, and the first, angled painting had to be redone.

What’s next

What’s built, and what isn’t yet

Built

In use by a few friends

  • All seven rooms, with accounts and syncing for invited members.
  • The bronze observatory, the Grimoire book and the temple.
  • A guest tour with a sample profile, where nothing is saved.

Look around as a guest, or read what’s inside.

Planned

Designed, not built

  • A constellation dome for the observatory.
  • A courtyard you pan across to reach each room, through doors that open.
  • Sound. The vision calls every action “an auditory and visual experience”, and so far only a few things make a sound.

It’s a private tool for a small circle, so I don’t have usage numbers worth quoting. What I have is a list of things my friends asked for, and the versions in which each one arrived.

My part: product design and direction, and the build, with AI as a build partner. The paintings are AI-generated from my briefs, then cut and fitted into the interface.
