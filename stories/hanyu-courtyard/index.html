How Hanyu Courtyard got its shape

Hanyu Courtyard is a Mandarin-learning adventure in private playtesting. These are the choices three playthroughs forced on it.

About a 4 minute read · Updated November 2026

  • The problem

The problem

Characters taught as pictures to memorise

I wanted to understand Chinese characters and put words together into sentences. The courses I tried split that into two jobs. A story happened over here, and the studying happened somewhere else, in a list.

A character taught as a small picture gives you nothing to reason from the next time you meet one of its parts. And a review list has no reason to exist beyond itself, so it’s the first thing I stop doing.

So the question for this game was whether the story could ask for each word before the lesson teaches it, and whether review could build something I cared about.

Key decisions

Four choices that shaped it

01

The story asks, the lesson answers

The model I borrowed is an old one from children’s learning games: a stretch of story, then a learning activity that the story needs, then more story. You inherit a neglected estate and meet Zhou Ming, the groundskeeper, who greets you in Mandarin before you know a word of it.

That greeting is the lesson’s reason to exist. You learn 你好 because someone just said it to you and is waiting for an answer.

02

Throw out the map I had just built

The first walkable version was a top-down map made of tiles, the kind you step across square by square. I built it, looked at it, and rejected it the same day. It read as a board game, and the estate needed to feel like somewhere you’d want to restore.

The replacement is closer to an old point-and-click mystery: wide painted scenes you slide across, with your character walking to wherever you click. Before committing, I made a small test page that showed the same courtyard from several camera heights and let me walk a figure across each. A low angle of about fifteen degrees won.

03

Cut the first unit by more than half

The first complete version of unit one taught 36 characters, each typed three ways and drawn from memory. On paper that came to 108 typing prompts and 72 drawings. I played it and it was far too long for a first sitting.

So I cut it to 15 characters in sentences of four characters or fewer, and changed how a new one arrives. Each now gets four short screens of its own: its parts and where they came from, its tone, its stroke order, and then your own attempt over a faint outline.

04

Let the handwriting stay yours

The game checks handwriting with an open-source tool called Hanzi Writer, which knows the correct order and direction of every stroke. In my first version, a correct stroke snapped into the textbook shape the moment you finished it.

After the second playtest I took that out, because it meant the character on the page was never really yours. Now your strokes stay exactly as you painted them, in the tone’s colour. A wrong one flashes and fades so you can try again, and a finished character blooms and moves on without a “Next” screen.

What’s next

The part that isn’t built is the point of the game

The idea the whole design leans on is that daily review restores the estate. Each visit you choose between reviewing at the desk or doing a household job, and either one earns the timber and stone that bring a room back. Missing a day costs nothing. Nothing you’ve earned is ever taken away.

That loop is designed and not yet playable, and I’d rather say so here than let the pictures imply otherwise.
