Design story: learning design and game design

Why I built my own Mandarin-learning game

Hanyu Courtyard is a Mandarin-learning adventure in private playtesting. I built it because the app I was using didn’t feel like learning.

A 6-minute read. Updated November 2026.

  • Why I built it
  • The game around the study
  • Four decisions
  • Before and after
  • What’s next

The app I used didn’t feel like learning

I wanted to learn Mandarin, and I had been using Duolingo. After many days of it, the app didn’t feel rigorous enough. I couldn’t point to anything I had learned.

I knew Chinese is a complex language. I had to focus on tones, and on learning how to write characters. The grammar is different, and so is the way of thinking. So I decided to build my own tools for it.

I also wanted a reason to study every day. I tied the tools to a video game: the more you study and do your daily work, the more you can help a man with his estate.

An estate that grows as you learn

It plays something like Animal Crossing. The more you study and use the language, the more you build up the place you’re working on, so you can see your estate grow with every bit of Chinese you learn. That gave me a visual way to show progress, and most progress in a language is review, because so much of learning is going back over what you already know.

So the main way to earn income in the game is your daily review. Reviewing keeps a streak, and a streak matters because a language needs exposure every single day, whether the material is new or old.

I set the game in a mythical China. That gave me room to be flexible and creative with a story for every grammar point. The game runs on quests that build up the estate, and the quests teach the words you need to work through HSK grammar. HSK is the standard Mandarin proficiency test, so everything ties to a framework that tells you what you’re learning and why.

Four decisions from three playtests

01

The story asks, the lesson answers

The model I borrowed is an old one from children’s learning games: a stretch of story, then a learning activity that the story needs, then more story. You inherit a neglected estate and meet Zhou Ming, the groundskeeper, who greets you in Mandarin before you know a word of it.

That greeting is the lesson’s reason to exist. You learn 你好 because someone just said it to you and is waiting for an answer.

02

Dropping the map I had just built

The first walkable version was a top-down map made of tiles, the kind you step across square by square. I built it, looked at it, and rejected it the same day. It read as a board game, and the estate needed to feel like somewhere you’d want to restore.

The replacement is closer to an old point-and-click mystery: wide painted scenes you slide across, with your character walking to wherever you click. Before committing, I made a small test page that showed the same courtyard from several camera heights and let me walk a figure across each. A low angle of about fifteen degrees won.

03

Cutting the first unit by more than half

The first complete version of unit one taught 36 characters, each typed three ways and drawn from memory. On paper that came to 108 typing prompts and 72 drawings. I played it and it was far too long for a first sitting.

So I cut it to 15 characters in sentences of four characters or fewer, and changed how a new one arrives. Each now gets four short screens of its own: its parts and where they came from, its tone, its stroke order, and then your own attempt over a faint outline.

04

Handwriting that stays yours

The game checks handwriting with an open-source tool called Hanzi Writer, which knows the correct order and direction of every stroke. In my first version, a correct stroke snapped into the textbook shape the moment you finished it.

After the second playtest I took that out, because it meant the character on the page was never really yours. Now your strokes stay exactly as you painted them, in the tone’s colour. A wrong one flashes and fades so you can try again, and a finished character blooms and moves on without a “Next” screen.

From pages you read to a place you walk

What isn’t built yet

The idea the whole design leans on is that daily review restores the estate. Each visit you choose between reviewing at the desk or doing a household job, and either one earns the timber and stone that bring a room back. Missing a day takes nothing away, and nothing you’ve earned is ever lost.

That loop is designed and not yet playable, so the pictures here show the first unit only.
