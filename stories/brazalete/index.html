Design story: game-based learning design

How I turned a conjugation chart into a Spanish grammar wristband

The Brazalete is a Spanish grammar tool for students who know the theory but struggle in execution.

My students said they knew the theory, but still stumbled when it came time to use it

I used to be a Spanish tutor. To help my students understand Spanish grammar, I gave them many resources they could study on their own.

They had every conjugation chart they needed, and most of them could recite the charts from memory. Then I’d ask them to form a sentence using the conjugations they said they knew. I would watch them stall halfway through, stop to think, and still get the sentence wrong.

Notes and charts share a weakness: they are passive. You read them, and nothing in them asks you to produce anything.

Passive and active learning

Passive learning means taking material in: reading notes, rereading a chart, listening to an explanation. Active learning means working with the material: answering questions, testing yourself, building a sentence from scratch.

Language skills divide the same way. Language teachers call reading and listening the receptive skills, because someone else already made the sentence and you recognise it. Speaking and writing are the productive skills, because you have to make the sentence yourself.

Receptive tools work well for reading and understanding, because you recognise what you studied. Productive work is harder, because you have to pull the words and the right form out of memory. If your brain hasn’t practised doing that, the sentence stalls. Think about introducing yourself in Spanish. The first time is hard. After a few hundred times you do it without thinking. The repetitions did that.

Exposure on its own doesn’t build the skill. You could watch someone lift weights every day with perfect technique and still not lift what they lift, because you never did the repetitions yourself.

Rereading feels like learning because the content looks familiar. Anyone who has speed-read their notes before an exam, instead of studying in a sustained and active way, knows how that goes. Having the resources and seeing them is not the same as being able to produce what they contain.

A 2006 study by Roediger and Karpicke put numbers on it. Students who tested themselves on the material remembered about 61% of it a week later. A control group that simply reread the material remembered about 40%.

I trained as a scientist, so I turned the problem into a hypothesis. If I gave each student a short daily exercise that forced them to produce the forms we were learning, would their test scores go up?

I tested it with students. In a playtest with 100 students, 87% scored higher on their exams after playing than before. The tutoring company ran the measurement.

The 2006 study covers self-testing in general. It says nothing about the Brazalete, which is why I ran my own test.

Six decisions

01

An object you wear

If reading was the problem, the answer had to be something you do with your hands. I asked my AI assistant for candidate objects and got eight back, among them a groovebox, a label maker, a combination lock and a dive computer. The round ones kept winning. A dial clicks into set positions, and a verb has a set form for each subject.

It also had to be something you wear. I never wanted a large object. I wanted technology people enjoy carrying, the way people carried Tamagotchis, and technology that helps its owner without taking over their life, like the Omnitrix in Ben 10 or a Yu-Gi-Oh card holder. That thinking took me to the Frutiger Aero era, when technology felt balanced and had a specific place in daily life, so I chose that look.

A wristband also has to be sleek. Anything with points sticking out would hook on a sleeve, so I kept the profile round with rounded corners. My first sketch had two small switches on the sides for positive and negative. They stuck out and I didn’t like them, so I turned them into a wheel. I also thought a lot about the circular doors in Skyrim, like the one that opens with the golden dragon claw.

I made the concept below with ChatGPT’s image generator, from a written description. I also built a rough working version in the browser. I wanted to know one thing before spending anything on art: is it fun to use?

I designed the Brazalete for The Scene, a Spanish-learning game. A character who forgets a word can’t pull out a grammar table without breaking the scene, so the table lives on something stylish the player wears.

02

A fixed spot for each subject

Every Spanish verb asks the same question first: who’s doing it? There are six answers, one for each subject, and on a chart they’re cells your eyes have to hunt for. I wanted each one to have a fixed spot you could find without looking.

So the wheel has six sections. Singular on the left, plural on the right, as in a normal grammar table. First person on top, second in the middle, third at the bottom. Yo sits straight across from nosotros.

All six are there, including vosotros, even though only Spain uses it. I wanted the tool to feel complete. The tú and vos switch has the same purpose. I didn’t want it to work only for Spanish from certain places.

Each section has its own color, because color helps the mind link things. Several small studies found that color coding helped learners remember German noun genders, and a University of Kansas dissertation reported better results for the color group. I haven’t found a study that tests color on Spanish verbs alone, so I treat it as a reasonable bet.

The look borrows from Simon and Bop It. People already enjoy pressing glowing buttons, so I copied that: chunky segments, dark edges, and a light that shows which subject you’ve chosen. The lighting tells a story too. A lit button means you can press it. A grey one means that form doesn’t exist for the mood and tense you picked.

03

Time runs left to right

I wanted the tenses on a line you could read as time. The preterite sits at the far left, because it describes one event that’s finished. The imperfect sits next to it. It describes a past that’s still unfolding or connected to the present, the way you tell a story.

The past participle sits just before the present and the gerund just after it. Each has only one form, so I kept them next to the present. If they lived somewhere else, you’d wonder why those buttons act differently from the rest. The conditional comes after the gerund and the future sits at the far right. I kept the layout symmetrical because an uneven one looked confusing.

Not every mood needs every tense. The imperative only needs one and the subjunctive only needs two, but I gave the subjunctive three. The third is the future subjunctive, which almost nobody uses in conversation today. You’ll still find it in older books, and I wanted learners who read to be able to look it up.

A small lever switches the past subjunctive between comiera and comiese. Both are correct. Learners often ask why there are two. Spanish has hundreds of millions of speakers across many regions, and languages shift with who’s speaking and where. I didn’t want the tool to pick a region for anyone.

04

All six forms, with labels you can swap

My first rule was never to show the answer. If the wheel displays every form, you can turn it until something looks right, and that’s recognising a word. Producing one is the harder skill. So the early wheel only showed who, and the form you’d dialled appeared on a small screen.

But a chart does one thing well. It shows the whole pattern together. I reversed the rule: all six forms stay lit every time you touch the wheel. You still choose the mood, the tense and the person yourself, and every repetition is also one more look at the full pattern.

Because the tool is digital, each section can change what it shows. It can show the pronoun with the full conjugated form, the pronoun alone so you have to recall the form, or only the ending. Endings work for regular verbs, since an -ar, -er or -ir verb follows a pattern. They miss stem changes like dormir, where duermo and duerme change the vowel. A list of endings would never quiz that.

Showing every form may cost some recall practice, but beginners see the full pattern far more often. As they advance, they can change what appears in each section of the wheel, so they have fewer training wheels.

05

The wheel goes dark

The whole device depends on six subjects. Then you reach comiendo, “eating”, and its partner, the past participle comido. Neither has a subject. I let the wheel go dark for both, because each has only one form and the other conjugations don’t affect it. Choose either one and all six sections switch off, and the centre lights up with the one form there is.

The same idea works in the other moods. The subjunctive has three tenses: present, imperfect and future. Four tense buttons go grey: the preterite, the conditional, the gerund and the participle. The imperfect subjunctive already covers the past, so it has no preterite. The conditional belongs to the indicative. The gerund and the participle never change with mood.

The imperative goes further. It only exists in the present, so every other tense button goes grey. Nobody commands themselves either, so the first-person section goes dark. There is no yo form to give. Negative commands borrow the subjunctive (come, but no comas), which is why the device has a NO switch: flip it in the imperative and the six forms change. A dark control shows where there is no form.

06

One layout for verbs and pronouns

English says “I buy it.” Spanish says “it I buy”, and “it” has a gender. That’s three decisions in one two-letter word: its job, its person and its gender. So pronouns got a second disc.

The disc copies the verb wheel’s layout: yo on the wheel, me in the same spot on the disc. One switch changes whether the pronoun is the direct object, the indirect object or reflexive. Learners struggle with those three because the names describe nothing you can picture. So I asked a different question: who does the verb happen to? I labelled the direct-object setting VÍCTIMA, the thing the verb acts on, and the question became “who’s the verb victim?” It also alliterates, which helps it stick.

Among the three jobs, only the third person changes. Me, te, nos and os stay the same. Third person splits into lo and la, le and se, so that’s where learners need the disc most.

Planned

Next

  • Ten-question quizzes that you answer on the device itself.
  • A timed mode set to music. A salsa groove plays and each sentence waits for you to answer inside the beat. It starts with the drums alone, and every right answer brings in another instrument. A wrong answer only drops the newest one out. Rhythm games like PaRappa the Rapper and Taiko no Tatsujin inspired it, because speaking needs conjugations to come fast.
  • A lesson that shows the preterite and the imperfect in two colors, followed by a past-tense quiz with no color hints. A subjunctive-trigger quiz shows its triggers in light purple to match the subjunctive dial.
  • Verb chains such as estar + gerund, haber + participle and no podrías haber ido (three verbs: poder, haber, ir), built one verb at a time.
  • A visual upgrade that brings the bronze frame from the concept art into the tool.
