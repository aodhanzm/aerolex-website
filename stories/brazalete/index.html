How a conjugation chart became something you wear

The brazalete is a Spanish grammar tool for the gap between knowing a rule and being able to use it.

About a 4 minute read · Updated November 2026

  • The problem
  • Five decisions
  • Before and after
  • What’s next

The problem

My students knew the chart and froze anyway

I used to tutor Spanish. My students had every conjugation chart they needed, and most of them could recite one. Then I’d ask for a sentence and watch them stall halfway through it.

A chart is something you read, and rereading feels like learning because the page looks familiar. In one well-known study, students who tested themselves remembered about 61% of the material a week later. The ones who reread it remembered about 40%.

So the question I started with was small and practical. Could I give each student homework that made them produce the forms they kept getting wrong, a little every day, in a way they would bother to do?

That study is about self-testing in general (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). It says nothing about this tool. I haven’t measured the brazalete against a chart, and I’d like to.

Key decisions

Five choices that shaped it

01

Make it an object you operate

If reading was the problem, the answer had to be something you do with your hands. I asked my AI assistant for candidate objects and got eight back, among them a groovebox, a label maker, a combination lock and a dive computer. The round ones kept winning. A dial has positions, and so does a verb.

The game this was first designed for has the player carrying words around, so a gadget in the hand meant no free hand. It moved to the wrist. I made the concept below with ChatGPT’s image generator, and built a rough working version in the browser at the same time to answer one question before spending anything on art: is it fun to turn?

02

Give every person a place

Every Spanish verb asks the same question first: who’s doing it? There are six answers, and on a chart they’re cells your eyes have to hunt for. I wanted each one to have a place your hand could learn.

So the wheel has six sections. Singular on the left, plural on the right. First person on top, second in the middle, third at the bottom. Yo sits straight across from nosotros. The look borrows from Simon, the memory game with the four fat glowing buttons: chunky segments, dark edges, and a light that means something when it’s on.

03

Break my own rule and show all six forms

My first rule was never to show the answer. If the wheel displays every form, you can turn it until something looks right, and that’s recognising a word. Producing one is the harder skill. So the early wheel only showed who, and the form you’d dialled appeared on a small screen.

But a chart does one thing well. It shows the whole pattern together. I reversed the rule: all six forms stay lit every time you touch the wheel. You still choose the mood, the tense and the person yourself, and every repetition is also one more look at the full pattern.

I’m probably trading some recall for a lot of exposure. For a beginner I think it’s the right trade. I could be wrong.

04

Let the wheel go dark

The whole device is built on six people. Then you reach comiendo, “eating”, which has no person at all. I could have hidden the wheel. I let it go dark instead: choose the gerund and all six sections switch off, and the centre lights up with the one form there is.

That idea spread. Switch to the subjunctive and the buttons that don’t apply go grey. Commands keep only the present. A small lever unfolds only for the one tense that comes in two versions, comiera and comiese. A dark control still teaches, because it shows you where the grammar has gaps.

05

Reuse the layout for pronouns

English says “I buy it.” Spanish says “it I buy”, and “it” has a gender. That’s three decisions hiding in a two-letter word, so pronouns got a second disc.

It copies the verb wheel’s layout on purpose: yo on the wheel, me in the same spot on the disc. One switch changes the pronoun’s job. I labelled the direct-object setting VÍCTIMA, the thing the verb happens to, because “direct object” is a term you memorise and a victim is something you can picture.

What’s next

What’s built, and what isn’t yet

Built

On this site now

  • The working device: 13 verbs, 10 adjectives and the pronoun disc.
  • Seven optional walkthroughs that use the real device.
  • The switch between tú and vos, with vosotros kept separate.

Try the brazalete. It’s free and needs no account.

Planned

Designed, not built

  • Quizzes of ten questions, answered with the device itself.
  • Two-part verbs such as estar + gerund and haber + participle.
  • A visual upgrade that brings the bronze frame from the concept art into the tool.

The fuller story, one decision at a time, is in the dev-log videos. Watch the dev log.

My part: the learning design and the interaction design. I built it with AI as a build partner, which for me means I decide what it should do and why, question every proposal, and test the result by using it.
